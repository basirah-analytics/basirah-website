Profitability Analysis: Why a Growing Business Was Quietly Losing Money

Every owner knows the feeling of a business that looks busy and successful from the outside. Sales are up, the shops are full, new accounts keep signing. And yet somehow there is never more money in the bank. This is a demonstration project we built to show how we find the answer to that exact problem. It uses a realistic simulated dataset for a specialty coffee roaster in Portland, with cafes, an online store, and a wholesale side, covering four years of sales down to the individual line item. The business is not a real client, but the patterns, rising costs, heavy discounting, and slow-paying customers, are the real ones that quietly drain profit from growing businesses.

The Problem

On paper, everything looked fine. Revenue climbed every year, the cafes were busy, and wholesale kept signing new accounts. But the owner had a nagging feeling he could not prove: he was selling more than ever, yet never seeing more money in the bank, and some months were tight even when the sales numbers looked great. He had three specific worries and no way to test them. That some products might be selling for less than they cost, without him knowing which ones. That the constant promotions and wholesale deals were costing more than they returned. And that wholesale customers were slow to pay, though he could not tell if it was a real pattern or just a few accounts he happened to remember. He did not have a data team. He had a point-of-sale system full of transactions, and he needed someone to look at all of it and tell him the truth.

The Questions We Set Out to Answer

  • Are we actually making money, and if not, why not?
  • Which products or categories are draining profit?
  • How much are discounts really costing us?
  • Which customers pay late, and is it a real risk?

What We Analyzed

We worked with four years of transaction-level data, more than a million sales line items, for a business selling across cafes, an online store, and wholesale accounts. The data was profiled and cleaned in SQL Server first: duplicate rows removed, fat-finger quantity errors quarantined, missing discounts filled, free comped items flagged, and inconsistent customer and city names standardized, with integrity checks confirming every record linked up correctly. From the clean data we built analytical views for line-level profit, monthly profit against operating costs, product profitability, location performance, and customer payment behavior, then brought it all together in a three-page Power BI report: an executive summary, a profit-leak deep dive, and a customers-and-payments view.

What We Found

On the surface, the business looked healthy. Four years of rising sales, around $31.4M in total revenue, and a gross margin holding near 35%. But the headline number told the real story: operating profit was negative, at roughly minus $760K. The business was making money on each sale and still losing money overall, because costs were climbing faster than revenue. The cost of goods rose steadily year after year, from around $3.3M in 2022 to $7.1M in 2025, while the margin line drifted downward. Selling more was not the fix. It was quietly making the problem bigger. Underneath that, three specific leaks were draining the profit.

The first leak was products sold below cost. About 22,000 order lines over the four years were sold for less than they cost to make. Those lines brought in $1.38M of revenue but lost around $228K once the true cost was counted, about 4.39% of revenue draining quietly away. The biggest single culprit was a surprise: the Large Macchiato, losing far more than any other product at around $10K, well ahead of the Coaster Set at $3.4K, with a handful of beans and equipment lines behind them. A popular everyday drink, not an obscure item, was being sold at a loss.

The second leak was discounting. As discounts got deeper, margin did not just dip, it collapsed. Orders with no discount held a healthy 43% margin. By the 21 to 30% discount tier, margin fell to just 13%, roughly a third of what it started at, and it dropped at every tier in between. The promotions that felt like they were moving product were quietly handing away the margin that keeps the business alive.

The third leak was cash flow, and it was hiding in plain sight. The business had around 16,000 unpaid invoices, paid on average about 15 days late. At first glance retail looked like the problem, with the highest count of unpaid invoices at roughly 10,900. But retail's late rate was near zero, that volume was just normal short-cycle billing. The real risk sat in wholesale, where about 80% of invoices were paid late across restaurant, cafe, and office accounts. That was the money tied up and out of reach, the working capital the owner could feel was missing even though the sales looked fine.

The Result

In three dashboard pages, the owner could finally see what the spreadsheets had hidden: that the business was busy but not profitable, and exactly where the money was leaking. Three clear actions came out of it. Re-price the products sold below cost, starting with the Large Macchiato. Cap the deepest discount tiers that were halving margin. Tighten wholesale payment terms to free up the cash trapped in late invoices. Not a data science lecture, just a short list of things to fix. That is the difference between having data and understanding it.
